What AI product engineering is

The model is the easy part.

AI product engineering is the work of turning a language model, or another AI capability, into software that real users can depend on. Calling a model is one line of code. The product is everything around it: what information the model sees, how its answers are checked, what happens when it is wrong, what each request costs, how fast it responds, and how it behaves when someone tries to misuse it.

That is different from general custom software development, because AI output is probabilistic. The same input can produce different answers, quality can drift when a provider updates a model, and costs scale with usage in ways traditional software does not. Those properties have to be designed for from the first release.

BuildWise Group builds and runs its own AI products, so we have made these trade-offs with our own money and our own users. We bring the same discipline to client products: a narrow first version, a way to measure whether it works, and an architecture that does not lock you into one model provider.

Engineering standards

What makes an AI product
production-ready.

Included in every AI product we build, scaled to the size of the project.

A

Quality you can measure

We define “good” before we build.

  • Evaluation sets built from real or realistic cases
  • Grounding and citations where answers must be factual
  • Regression checks when prompts or models change
  • Model choice per task, kept replaceable

B

Cost and performance

Unit economics that survive real usage.

  • Cost per request estimated and monitored
  • Caching, batching and smaller models where they suffice
  • Latency budgets for each user journey
  • Usage limits to prevent runaway spend

C

Security and data

AI features treated as an attack surface.

  • Untrusted input handled with prompt-injection defences
  • Least-privilege tool and data access
  • Data protection designed in (NDPA, GDPR where relevant)
  • Independent review available: security

How an engagement works

Prove it small.
Then build it properly.

AI projects fail when a demo is mistaken for a product. Each stage ends with evidence you can judge.

  1. 01

    Discovery

    The users, the job the product does for them, the data available, the risks, and what “working” means in measurable terms.

  2. 02

    Feasibility spike

    A short technical test on real examples to show whether the AI part can reach the quality needed, before the full build is committed.

  3. 03

    Scope and architecture

    A written scope for the first release, the key screens and an architecture that keeps models, data and costs under control.

  4. 04

    Build and evaluate

    Working software in short increments, with the evaluation set run on every significant change.

  5. 05

    Launch and operate

    A controlled launch, monitoring of quality, cost and errors, and improvements based on how people actually use it.

Frequently asked questions

Questions, answered plainly.

What does an AI product engineer do?

Designs and builds the software around an AI model: what data it sees, how its output is checked and shown to users, how costs and speed are controlled, and how the product stays secure. The model is one component; the product is the system.

Can you build our MVP?

Yes. We scope a focused first release that real users can try, usually after a short feasibility test on the AI part so you know it can reach the quality you need.

Which AI models and providers do you use?

We choose per task from established providers, weighing quality, speed, cost and where data is processed, and we design the product so the model can be changed without a rebuild.

How do you know if the AI is good enough?

We build an evaluation set from real or realistic cases and agree the quality bar with you before launch. The same set is rerun when prompts or models change, so quality does not quietly drift.

Can you take over an AI prototype someone else built?

Yes. We start with a review of the code, prompts, data handling, costs and security, tell you what we find, then propose a plan to make it production-ready.

Who owns the product?

For software we build for you, ownership is set out in the contract. Our default is that you own what you pay us to build.
